📈 Probability Distributions

Learn how probability distributions describe random variables, which formulas to use, and how to solve exam-style questions.

1. What is a probability distribution?

A probability distribution describes the possible values of a random variable and the probabilities associated with them.

A random variable is a numerical outcome of a random experiment. For example, X could represent the number of heads obtained when three coins are tossed.

Two main types

  • Discrete distribution: Used when a variable takes separate, countable values, such as 0, 1, 2, or 3 defective items.
  • Continuous distribution: Used when a variable can take any value within an interval, such as a person's height or the time taken to complete a task.

2. The Binomial Distribution

The binomial distribution counts the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials.

Conditions for using it

  • The number of trials, n, is fixed.
  • Each trial has two outcomes: success or failure.
  • The probability of success, p, stays constant.
  • The trials are independent.
P(X = x) = C(n, x) px(1 − p)n − x

Where:

  • n: Number of trials.
  • x: Number of successes.
  • p: Probability of success.
  • 1 − p: Probability of failure.
  • C(n, x): Number of ways to select x successes from n trials.
Mean = np
Variance = np(1 − p)

Worked example

A fair coin is tossed three times. Find the probability of obtaining exactly two heads.

Step 1: Identify the values.

n = 3, x = 2, p = 0.5

Step 2: Apply the binomial formula.

P(X = 2) = C(3, 2)(0.5)2(0.5)1

Step 3: Calculate.

C(3, 2) = 3

P(X = 2) = 3 × 0.25 × 0.5 = 0.375

Answer: The probability is 0.375, or 37.5%.

3. The Poisson Distribution

The Poisson distribution models the number of times an event occurs in a fixed interval of time, space, or another measure, when events occur at a constant average rate.

P(X = x) = (e−λ λx) / x!

Where:

  • λ (lambda): Average number of events in the interval.
  • x: Number of events whose probability is required.
  • e: Mathematical constant, approximately 2.71828.
  • x!: Factorial of x.
Mean = λ
Variance = λ

Worked example

A shop receives an average of two customer complaints per day. Assuming a Poisson distribution, find the probability of receiving exactly one complaint on a particular day.

Step 1: Identify the values.

λ = 2 and x = 1

Step 2: Substitute into the formula.

P(X = 1) = (e−2 × 21) / 1!

Step 3: Calculate.

P(X = 1) ≈ 0.2707

Answer: The probability is approximately 0.2707, or 27.07%.

4. The Normal Distribution

The normal distribution is a continuous, bell-shaped probability distribution. It is symmetric around its mean.

It is described by two parameters:

  • μ (mu): Population mean.
  • σ (sigma): Population standard deviation.
Z = (X − μ) / σ

This formula converts an observation X into a standard score, showing how many standard deviations it lies above or below the mean.

Worked example

Exam scores have a mean of 70 and a standard deviation of 10. Find the standard score for a student who scores 85.

Step 1: Identify the values.

X = 85, μ = 70, σ = 10

Step 2: Substitute.

Z = (85 − 70) / 10
Z = 15 / 10 = 1.5

Answer: The score is 1.5 standard deviations above the mean.

5. How to choose the correct distribution

  • Binomial: A fixed number of independent trials with two possible outcomes.
  • Poisson: Counting events in an interval when an average rate is given.
  • Normal: Modelling continuous measurements with a bell-shaped distribution.
Exam tip: Identify what the question is counting or measuring before choosing a formula. The wording often tells you which distribution is appropriate.

6. Practice questions

  1. A fair coin is tossed four times. Find the probability of obtaining exactly three heads.
  2. A call centre receives an average of three calls per minute. Assuming a Poisson distribution, find the probability of receiving zero calls in a particular minute.
  3. A normal distribution has mean 100 and standard deviation 15. Find the Z-score for an observation of 130.
  4. State two conditions required for a binomial distribution.

Try solving these questions before checking your textbook or lecture notes.
